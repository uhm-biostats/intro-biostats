Normal distribution

The normal distribution is a continuous probability distribution describing a bell-shaped curve. It is a good approximation to the frequency distribution of many biological variables.

The normal distribution has two parameters, mean and standard deviation

For the population, consider a continuous random variable \(Y\) with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\)

\[f(Y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \sigma^2}} e ^ {\frac{-(Y - \mu) ^ 2}{2 \sigma ^ 2}}\] \(f(Y)\) is the probability density of \(Y\). Higher probability when:

  • \(Y\) is close to \(\mu\) -0The spread of the distribution is low (\(\sigma\) is small)

The normal distribution has two parameters, mean and standard deviation

Properties of a normal distribution

  • Continuous distribution
  • Symmetrical around its mean
  • It has a single mode
  • The probability density is highest exactly at the mean

Discrete versus Continuous distributions

Discrete versus Continuous distributions

Discrete: Probability of 5 successes is 0.25

Continuous: Probability of \(-0.32 < Y < 0.32\) is 0.25

Symmetrical around its mean

Symmetrical around its mean

Asymmetric: Probability of \(\chi^2 > \text{mean}\) is 0.42

Symmetric: Probability of \(Y > \text{mean}\) is 0.5

It has a single mode

Standard error of the sample mean

The standard error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution

If we take a sample of \(n\) from a normally distributed population with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the population standard error of the sample mean is:

\[\sigma_{\overline{Y}} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\]

Standard error of the sample mean is \(\sigma_{\overline{Y}} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\)

The probability density of a sample mean

If \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\), what’s the probability of the sample mean \(\overline{Y} = 1.0\) for \(n = 4\)?

Trick question: with a continuous variable, the probability of any exact value of \(\overline{Y}\) is infinitesimal (\(\approx 0\)).

The probability density of a sample mean

If \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\), what’s the probability density of the sample mean \(\overline{Y} = 1.0\) for \(n = 4\)?

We can answer this!

In R

dnorm(1.0, mean = 0, sd = 1 / sqrt(4))
[1] 0.1079819

This does NOT mean there is a 10% chance of \(\overline{Y} = 1.0\)

The probability density of a range of sample means

If \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\), what’s the probability density that the sample mean \(\overline{Y} \ge 1.0\) for \(n = 4\)?

Trick question: probability density is associated with a specific value of \(\overline{Y}\).

The probability of a range of sample means

If \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\), what’s the probability that the sample mean \(\overline{Y} \ge 1.0\) for \(n = 4\)?

We can answer this!

In R

pnorm(1.0, mean = 0, sd = 1 / sqrt(4), lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.02275013

This means there is a 2.28% chance of \(\overline{Y} \ge 1.0\)

The probability of a range of sample means

The probability density \(f(Y)\) associated with \(\overline{Y}\) is the height of the curve

The probability of a range of sample means in the area under the curve for that range

\(Z\)-transformation: transform any normal distribution to a standard normal

\[Z = \frac{Y - \mu}{\sigma}\]

\(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 2\)

\(Y\) \(Z\)
-1 \(\frac{-1 - 1}{2} = -1\)
1 \(\frac{1 - 1}{2} = 0\)
3 \(\frac{3 - 1}{2} = 1\)

\(\mu = -2\), \(\sigma = 3\)

\(Y\) \(Z\)
-5 \(\frac{-5 - (-2)}{3} = -1\)
-2 \(\frac{-2 - (-2)}{3} = 0\)
1 \(\frac{1 - (-2)}{3} = 1\)

\(Z\): standard normal deviate

A standard normal deviate, or \(Z\), tells us how many standard deviations a particular value is from the mean

Central Limit Theorem

According to the central limit theorem, the sum or mean of a large number of measurements randomly sampled from a non-normal population is approximately normally distributed.

Why the Central Limit Theorem matters

  1. It explains why so many quantities are normally distributed
  2. When \(n\) is large, many statistical tests that assume data are normal sampling distributions work OK
  3. It can speed up calculations for large samples from non-normal distributions
